050 01

The Atmosphere

Composition, layers, ISA, and everything altimetry. This topic and Flight Hazards together carry the most marks in a selection paper.

Composition

  • Nitrogen 78 %, Oxygen 21 %, Argon ~0.9 %, CO₂ ~0.03 %. Constant to ~80 km.
  • Water vapour is the variable constituent and the single most important one for weather — it carries latent heat and is lighter than dry air, so moist air is less dense.
  • Ozone peaks 20–50 km. At high cruise levels it can cause eye and skin irritation — the classic "scratching eyes in cruise" question.

Layers

  • Troposphere — all weather, temp falls with height. Depth varies with latitude.
  • Stratosphere — to ~50 km. Stable, few clouds, temperature rises (ozone absorption).
  • Mesosphere to ~80 km, Thermosphere to ~500 km, then exosphere.
  • The tropopause is the lid: higher = colder, because the air keeps cooling all the way up to it.

Altimetry — the whole toolkit

  • QFE — reads 0 ft on the airfield.
  • QNH — reads airfield elevation on the ground; QFE reduced to MSL using ISA.
  • QFF — reduced to MSL using actual conditions. Used for surface synoptic charts. Warmer than ISA → QFF < QNH; colder than ISA → QFF > QNH.
  • QNE — the altimeter reading at touchdown with 1013.25 set.
  • Airfield below sea level → QFE is greater than QNH.
  • 1 hPa = 30 ft near MSL (27 ft is the strict surface value); the interval grows with height — ~50 ft at 500 hPa, ~60 ft at 375 hPa / FL250.
  • Pressure halves every ~18 000 ft / 5 500 m.
  • Isobar = equal pressure at a given level. Isohypse / contour = true altitude of a pressure level. Isotach = equal wind speed. Isotherm = equal temperature.
  • Flying from high to low pressure or high to low temperature → true altitude is lower than indicated. High to low, look out below.

Temperature error correction — the 4 % rule
Correction (ft) = 4 × (height in 1000s of ft) × |ΔT from ISA|
True altitude = Indicated altitude − correction  (when colder than ISA)

Example: 16 000 ft indicated, OAT −32 °C.
ISA at 16 000 ft = 15 − 32 = −17 °C  →  ΔT = −32 − (−17) = −15 °C
Correction = 4 × 16 × 15 = 960 ft  →  True altitude = 15 040 ft
Selection trap

Pressure error and temperature error are two separate corrections. A QNH question (1013 vs local) uses 30 ft per hPa. A cold-weather question uses the 4 % rule. Read which one they are asking for before you start.

050 02

Wind

Three forces make every wind question: pressure gradient, Coriolis, friction. Get the balance right and the local winds and shear answers fall out.

The force balance

  • Pressure Gradient Force (PGF) — always from high to low, perpendicular to the isobars. Closer isobars = stronger wind.
  • Coriolis — deflects right in the NH, left in the SH. C = 2 · V · Ω · sin φ: proportional to speed and to sine of latitude. Zero at the equator, maximum at the poles.
  • Geostrophic wind — PGF balanced by Coriolis, blowing parallel to straight isobars and therefore perpendicular to the PGF. Valid above ~2000 ft and away from the equator.
  • For the same pressure gradient, geostrophic wind is stronger at low latitude (weaker Coriolis needs more speed to balance) — so 30°N > 60°N.
  • Gradient wind — add centrifugal force for curved isobars: sub-geostrophic around a low, super-geostrophic around a high.
  • Friction layer — surface to ~2000 ft. Friction slows the wind, which weakens Coriolis, so the wind backs across the isobars towards the low (NH).
  • Land: backs ~30°, speed ~50 % of the 2000 ft wind. Sea: ~10°, ~70 %.
  • Therefore in the NH: descending through the friction layer the wind backs and decreases; climbing out it veers and increases.
  • Surface wind is measured at 10 m and reported in °True in METAR/TAF, but passed in °Magnetic by ATC.
  • Buys Ballot (NH): back to the wind → low pressure on your left.

Thermodynamics & Stability

Humidity, adiabatic lapse rates, and the single comparison that decides everything: ELR against the adiabatic rate.

Cloud base (Cu) estimate(T − Td) × 400 ft

Humidity — the three questions they ask

  • Relative humidity = actual water vapour ÷ maximum the air could hold at that temperature. Warm air holds more.
  • Heat the air without adding moisture → RH decreases, dew point unchanged. Cool it → RH increases. This is the whole of three separate bank questions.
  • Descending air warms adiabatically → RH decreases → cloud dissolves. This is why anticyclones are cloud-free.
  • Unsaturated air is brought to saturation by expanding it adiabatically (i.e. lifting it) or by cooling it to its dew point.

Stability — read it off the lapse rates

ConditionTestWeather you get
Absolutely stableELR < SALRLayer cloud, poor visibility, steady precipitation, smooth
Conditionally unstableSALR < ELR < DALRUnstable only once saturated — the usual mid-latitude case
Absolutely unstableELR > DALRCumuliform, showers, turbulence, good visibility

Heating from below destabilises (cold air over a warm surface: cumulus, turbulence, good visibility, clear ice). Cooling from below stabilises (warm air over a cold surface: ground inversion, fog, layer cloud, poor visibility). Warm air advected aloft over cold air below also increases stability.

Inversions

  • An inversion is temperature increasing with height. It is the definition of stability and it caps convection — mid-latitude cumulus tops are usually limited by one.
  • Surface / radiation inversion: terrestrial radiation on a clear, calm night, best over dry ground in winter.
  • Subsidence inversion: sinking air in an anticyclone warms adiabatically aloft.
  • The greatest low-level wind shear is at the top of a surface-based inversion — calm below, gradient wind above.
  • Descending through one in winter with no pilot input: warmer, less dense air → thrust increases, and you enter the trapped pollution → visibility deteriorates.

050 05

Precipitation

A short topic, but the "which cloud gives which precipitation" logic recurs everywhere.

  • Layer cloud → continuous or intermittent precipitation (rain, snow, drizzle). Convective cloud → showers.
  • Drizzle (DZ) comes from thick stratus only — small droplets, closely packed, and it is the least likely product of a mixed-phase cloud. It reduces visibility more than rain.
  • In a cold cloud (ice crystals + supercooled water) the Bergeron process runs: crystals grow at the droplets' expense, so snow forms first.
  • Virga / fallstreaks — precipitation evaporating before reaching the ground. It is a visual LLWS warning: something is falling and cooling the air.
  • Hail — transparent or partly opaque solid precipitation, ≥5 mm; needs strong updraughts, so effectively a Cb product. In an isolated temperate Cb it can be found from ground to FL450 and can be thrown out of the anvil into clear air.
  • Ice pellets (PL) — transparent, ≤5 mm, frozen raindrops. Evidence of an inversion aloft: rain fell through a sub-zero layer.
  • Freezing rain (FZRA) — supercooled rain freezing on contact. Warm front, low levels; probability is lowest at high altitude.
  • Visibility is worse in wet snow than in rain; better in RA than in DZ.

Air Masses & Fronts

The heaviest scoring topic in most selection papers. Learn the two cross-sections and the occlusion rule and you can answer almost anything here.

Air masses

Classified by source latitude (Arctic / Polar / Tropical) and surface (Maritime / Continental). The surface it crosses on the way determines the weather it delivers.

Air massCharacterWhere you meet it
Polar Maritime (PM)Cool, moist, conditionally unstable — showers, good visibilityThe most common over W/NW Europe
Arctic Maritime (AM)Very cold, unstableNortherly outbreaks
Polar Continental (PC)Cold, dry, stable — winter only over EuropeEasterly flow from Russia
Tropical Maritime (TM)Warm, moist, stable — stratus, advection fog, drizzleAzores High; the warm sector air

The warm front sequence, in order

  • Ahead: Ci → Cs (halo) → As → Ns. Pressure falls. Wind backs slightly. Continuous rain, then very low stratus formed when the falling rain saturates the cold air. Frontal fog. Most of the significant surface weather occurs before the passage.
  • At passage: wind veers, pressure steadies, temperature rises, precipitation eases.
  • Warm sector: tropical maritime air — stratus, drizzle, poor visibility, advection fog risk. Fog risk is greater at the warm front than at the cold front.
  • Cold front passage: pressure had been rising just before it and then falls after? No — the classic: pressure falls ahead of the whole depression, reaches its minimum in the warm sector, and rises sharply behind the cold front. Wind veers sharply, temperature drops, visibility improves, showers.
  • Warm-front thunderstorms are possible if the warm air is moist and the ELR exceeds the SALR (i.e. the overrunning air is itself unstable).

050 07

Pressure Systems

Convergence and divergence: what happens at the surface is set by what the upper flow is doing.

Cyclone (low) — NH

  • Rotation anticlockwise, flow convergent at the surface and rising, divergent aloft.
  • Rising air → cooling → cloud, precipitation, poor weather, strong winds.
  • When upper divergence exceeds surface convergence: pressure falls and cloud forms — the depression deepens.

Anticyclone (high) — NH

  • Rotation clockwise, subsiding air, divergent at the surface.
  • Subsidence inversion, dry aloft, little cloud, light winds; in winter, trapped haze and fog under the inversion.
  • Warm anticyclone — warm through its depth, pressure surfaces bulge up at all levels, extends to the tropopause, quasi-stationary, fine weather dominates in summer.
  • Cold anticyclone — shallow, pressure surfaces bulge up at low level and down aloft; typically a winter continental high (Siberian).
  • Blocking high — a quasi-stationary warm anticyclone between 50°N and 70°N that diverts depressions around it.
  • Cold air drop (cut-off low) — a pool of cold air detached from the polar flow. Most evident in the mid-troposphere with little surface indication: no fronts, but a deep unstable column giving showers and thunderstorms from a "quiet" surface chart.
  • Col — between two highs and two lows: light variable winds, fog in winter, thunderstorms in summer.
  • Trough extends low pressure, ridge extends high pressure.

050 08

Climatology

Mostly recall. Learn the tropical revolving storm names, the monsoon directions, and the ITCZ position by season.

ITCZ & the tropics

  • The ITCZ follows the sun with a lag: north in July, south in January. It sits over Africa/Asia, not over the North Atlantic — a London–New York summer crossing never crosses it.
  • Equatorial precipitation: showers and thunderstorms all year, peaking around the equinoxes — April–May and October–November.
  • Main icing zone within the ITCZ: FL160–FL260.

Monsoon

  • Summer (India): SW monsoon — hot land, moist air off the ocean, heavy rain.
  • Winter: NE monsoon — dry continental outflow.

Tropical revolving storms

  • Need sea surface ≥ 26–27 °C, latitude ≥ 5° (Coriolis), and occur in late summer / autumn — never winter.
  • Most frequent region: the NW Pacific (typhoons).
  • Names: Hurricane (Atlantic/E Pacific), Typhoon (NW Pacific), Cyclone (Indian Ocean), Willy-Willy (Australia).
  • Seen from a distance the approach is announced by frequent stratocumulus and a long swell.

Mediterranean

  • Hot dry summers, mild wet winters, annual rainfall well below 700 mm.

Flight Hazards

Icing, thunderstorms, wind shear, mountain waves, CAT. Together with altimetry this is where the marks are.

Icing

Icing temperature bands

Mixed ice−10 °C → −20 °C

No icingabove 0 °C, below −40/−45 °C

Highest icing risk cloudCb, then Ns
  • Clear ice — large supercooled droplets that spread before freezing. Transparent, heavy, adheres beyond the de-ice boots, hard to shed. The most dangerous. Cumuliform cloud and freezing rain.
  • Rime ice — small droplets that freeze instantly and do not spread. Opaque, brittle, follows the leading edge. Stratiform cloud.
  • Icing risk is highest in Ns among layer clouds (great depth, high water content) and virtually ruled out in Cs and other high cloud — ice crystals do not stick.
  • Carburettor icing is worst at +2 °C with 90 % RH — a warm, humid day, not a freezing one.

Thunderstorms

Three ingredients: unstable lapse rate (ELR > SALR through a great depth), high humidity, and an initial lifting trigger (heating, orographic, frontal, or convergence). For air-mass storms over the sea, that trigger is cold air advection over a warm sea.

Cb avoidance — the exam answers

Avoid by at least 10 NM laterally (20 NM in the tropics or above FL250), and overfly only by 5000 ft above the top. If you cannot circumnavigate a Cb, fly above it if performance allows — never below, never through. Airborne weather radar detects precipitation, so it under-reads dry hail and the anvil; use it to avoid, not to penetrate.

Wind shear

  • Vertical wind shear = the vertical variation of the horizontal wind, measured in kt per 100 ft (ICAO: moderate 4.0–7.9, severe 8.0–11.9, extreme ≥12).
  • Horizontal wind shear = change of the horizontal wind with horizontal distance at the same level.
  • Sources: thunderstorm outflow and microbursts, low-level temperature inversions (greatest shear at the top of a surface-based inversion), frontal surfaces, sea breeze fronts, and terrain. At night, expect it with radiation inversions.
  • Virga is the visual cue. A microburst arises in the downdraught of a Cb in the mature stage: diameter under 4 km, life under 15 min, downdraughts of 3000–4000 ft/min, and it can be dry — no rain reaching the ground at all.
  • Reported as WS ALL RWY or WS RWY nn in a METAR; a wind shear warning (e.g. MBST APCH RWY26) is updated every 1 minute.

Mountain waves & CAT

  • Standing waves need wind ≥20–25 kt perpendicular to the ridge (±30°), increasing with height, and a stable layer at ridge level.
  • Markers: lenticularis at the wave crests, cap cloud on the summit, rotor cloud in the turbulent zone. The most hazardous conditions are in the rotor zone beneath the first wave crest on the lee side.
  • CAT sits around the jet core: strongest on the cold-air side and just below the core, and in sharp upper troughs. Fastest escape from CAT near a jet: descend (or turn towards the warm air).

Rules of observation

  • Meteorological visibility in a METAR: the greatest value reached over at least half the horizon circle (with the minimum given separately if it differs significantly and is below 1500 m or 50 % of the prevailing visibility).
  • Winds are written in °True in METAR/TAF and spoken in °Magnetic by ATC — the single most common trap in this topic.
  • CAVOK requires: visibility ≥10 km, no cloud below 5000 ft or below the highest MSA (whichever is greater) and no CB or TCU at any level, and no significant weather. OVC150 still qualifies — 15 000 ft is above the limit.
  • SIGMET is issued for en-route phenomena hazardous to all aircraft: thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe mountain waves, severe icing, hail, volcanic ash, TRS, sandstorms, radioactive cloud. AIRMET is the lower-level equivalent for light aircraft.
  • Runway friction coefficient: 0.40+ good · 0.36–0.39 medium/good · 0.30–0.35 medium · 0.26–0.29 medium/poor · 0.25 and below ("friction coefficient 20") = poor.
  • In-flight sources: VOLMET, ATIS, and any ATS unit.
  • Significant weather charts: well-separated Cb are annotated OCNL CB; ISOL = individual, FRQ = little or no separation, EMBD = embedded in layer cloud.
