040 01

Altitude, Oxygen & Pressure

Everything here follows from one fact: pressure falls with height, and so does the partial pressure of oxygen.

Time of useful consciousness (sudden decompression)
FL18020–30 min
FL2503–5 min
FL3001–2 min
FL35030–60 s
FL40015–20 s
General gas lawP × V / T = constant

Hypoxia

  • Insufficient oxygen reaching the body tissues, caused by the reduced partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs. It impairs thinking and reactions.
  • Symptoms: euphoria, poor judgement, lack of concentration, fatigue, increased heart rate, cyanosis, somnolence, and blurred or tunnel vision.
  • The most dangerous symptom for flight safety is impaired judgement and loss of self-criticism — you feel fine while performing badly.
  • Night vision is the first function to suffer, from as low as 5000 ft.
  • Fatigue, stress and smoking all lower your tolerance to it. Above FL100 after a slow pressurisation failure the onset is gradual and hard to recognise.
  • Hypoxia can itself trigger hyperventilation.

Hyperventilation & decompression sickness

  • Hyperventilation is breathing beyond physiological need. It washes out CO₂, so the blood becomes more alkaline (acidity reduced), cerebral vessels constrict, and paradoxically less oxygen is released into the cells.
  • Triggers: anxiety, stress, pain, pressure breathing. Shared symptom with hypoxia: tingling in the arms and legs.
  • Decompression sickness = dissolved nitrogen coming out of solution in tissues and fluids. Bends = joint pain. Chokes = chest and lungs. It can also cause embolism.
  • Not symptoms of DCS: red-out and the leans — both are asked as traps.
  • Barotrauma comes from pressure differentials in body cavities — ears, sinuses, teeth, gut.

Circulation & Health

The physiology numbers, and the handful of medical facts the bank asks about.

  • Blood: red cells carry oxygen, white cells provide immunity, platelets coagulate.
  • A stroke occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is cut off; a heart infarction follows a blocked coronary artery.
  • Hypertension increases the risk of heart attack and stroke.
  • Alcohol is eliminated at roughly 0.015 % per hour and the rate cannot be accelerated — coffee, food and time-of-day make no difference.
  • Signals pass between neurons by the release of chemicals across the synapse.
  • +Gz (radial) acceleration has the most significant physiological effect on pilots.
  • Alpha radiation is stopped by a sheet of paper; beta needs metal, gamma needs lead.
  • Hepatitis A can be prevented by gamma-globulin or vaccination.
  • Contact lenses (Part-MED): you must carry a spare set of spectacles approved by the AME.
  • Caffeine is not only in coffee — hot chocolate contains it too.
  • Psychosomatic: mental or emotional stress can produce genuine organic reactions.

040 03

Vision

Rods and cones sit in different places, and that single fact explains night vision, the blind spot and off-centre viewing.

  • Light path: cornea → iris/pupil → lens → retina.
  • Rods: low light, no colour, night (scotopic) vision. Cones: sharp colour vision in bright light (photopic).
  • Preserve night vision by avoiding smoking and flash-blindness; full dark adaptation takes about 30 minutes.
  • Factors degrading visual acuity at altitude: autokinesis, carbon monoxide poisoning and hypoxia.
  • Empty-field myopia — with no distant focal points the eye relaxes to a near focus, so distant traffic goes unseen.
  • Cataract is a clouding of the lens.
  • Monocular depth cues: obscuration (interposition), texture gradient, atmospheric perspective, relative size and motion parallax. Binocular cues need both eyes and work only close in.
  • Flying into fog, snow or haze makes objects appear farther away than they are.

040 04

Vestibular System & Illusions

Your inner ear measures acceleration, not attitude. Every illusion in this topic comes from that limitation.

  • The vestibular apparatus consists of the semi-circular canals (angular acceleration) and the otoliths (linear acceleration and gravity). Together they measure linear and angular acceleration in the inner ear.
  • Somatogravic illusion — linear acceleration is felt as a change of pitch, giving a false impression of climbing or descending. Being pressed into the seat feels like starting a climb.
  • Proprioceptive senses ("seat of the pants") give false information once visual references are lost.
  • Autokinesis — a stationary light in darkness appears to move.
  • Flickering light from a propeller or rotor may cause disorientation or nausea.
  • Cure for vertigo: in IMC, establish an instrument cross-check, trust the instruments and ignore the sensations. In good visibility, look at the horizon.
  • Motion sickness: look at a solid point such as the horizon. Euphoria is not one of its symptoms.

Approach illusions

SituationYou feelYou tend to
Narrow runwayToo highFly low, flare too late
Wide runwayToo lowFly high, land long
Upslope runwayToo highLand short
Downslope runwayToo lowLand long

The pattern is consistent: whatever makes the runway look bigger makes you feel low, and whatever makes it look smaller makes you feel high — and you then fly the opposite of what you feel.

Sleep, Fatigue & Circadian Rhythms

Two kinds of sleep doing two different jobs, and why eastbound is worse.

Sleep & circadian numbers
Resynchronisation rate1–1.5 h per day
Harder directioneastbound
REM sleep restoresthe brain
Deep (slow-wave) sleep restoresthe body
Fatigue categoriesacute and chronic
  • REM (paradoxical) sleep re-vitalises the brain and is characterised by rapid eye movements and dreaming.
  • Deep sleep handles physical recovery.
  • Lack of sleep causes fatigue, reduced concentration, an increased risk of sensory illusions and mood disorders.
  • Jet lag: adaptation takes longer eastbound (you must shorten your day, which the body resists) and varies between individuals. Resynchronisation runs at 1 to 1.5 hours per day.
  • Causes of fatigue: sleep loss and jet lag. Acute fatigue is short-term and reversible with rest; chronic fatigue accumulates.
  • Acute fatigue symptoms: lack of vigilance, falling asleep, irritability and an increased error rate.

Information Processing

Perception, attention and the three memories — with the numbers they ask for.

Memory & attention

Minimum stimulus strengthsensory threshold

  • Working / short-term memory is limited in size, sensitive to interference and interruption, and lasts about 20 seconds.
  • Long-term memory's main limitation is the difficulty of retrieving what is stored, not storing it.
  • Divided attention is managing several matters sequentially — not simultaneously.
  • The cocktail party effect is the ability to pick up relevant information unintentionally, from a channel you were not attending to.
  • Motor programs are stored routines executed without conscious control — efficient, but they fire on cue.
  • Environmental capture is exactly that failure: applying a learned skill in an inappropriate context.
  • Mental models are generated by experience and learning.
  • Situational awareness is the perception, comprehension and projection of the environment in time and space — in short, perception matching reality.
  • Confirmation bias is ignoring information that contradicts a decision already made. Habits push you toward familiar rather than optimal solutions.
  • With a high workload on VNAV/LNAV, the way to keep situational awareness is to downgrade to simpler automation modes such as ALT/HDG.

040 07

Stress

Stress is not the enemy — too little of it is as bad as too much.

  • A stress reaction is the non-specific response of the body to any demand. A stressor is any internal or external stimulus perceived as stressful.
  • Perception of stress depends on subjective evaluation and your ability to cope — which is also how risk assessment works.
  • Environmental stressors: heat, humidity, noise. Stressors accumulate and raise the risk of exhaustion.
  • Cognitive effects: haste, fixation, mental block, more errors. Physical symptoms: insomnia, loss of appetite, dry mouth.

General Adaptation Syndrome

  1. Alarm — the body mobilises, adrenaline is released.
  2. Resistance — it adapts and copes at a cost.
  3. Exhaustion — resources run out and performance collapses.

Coping

  • Problem-focused coping acts on the stressor itself and is the one that reduces both physiological and psychological stress.
  • Cognitive coping works on your interpretation — ignoring or rationalising the stressor.
  • Stress management means recognising stress and developing coping strategies. The drawback of Stress Exposure Training is that totally unexpected situations cannot be generated.

040 08

Human Error & Threat and Error Management

The modern view: people will err, so build systems that tolerate it. TEM is the framework the exam wants.

Types of error

  • Human error is a mismatch between intention and outcome.
  • Active errors have immediate effects at the human-machine interface — the sharp end.
  • Latent errors are hidden and condition-dependent, sitting in the system and giving a misleading impression of safety.
  • An error-tolerant system is one where the consequences do not jeopardise safety.
  • The modern approach is to design systems that acknowledge human fallibility, and to improve reliability by understanding the causes of errors and how to recover from them.

Models & culture

  • SHELL: Software, Hardware, Environment, Liveware — with Liveware at the centre.
  • Swiss Cheese: an accident happens when the barriers momentarily align.
  • TEM threat categories: organisational (usually latent and controllable at source) and environmental (some predictable, some spontaneous, all managed in real time — a contaminated runway, for example).
  • A latent threat is not directly observable by the flight crew.
  • Just culture: honest errors are not punished, but violations are not tolerated. Safety culture is promoted when upper management provides the required resources.
  • In complex organisations, the safety manager and a safety review board hold responsibility for flight safety.

040 09

CRM, Leadership & Communication

The crew as a system: what makes two pilots better than two individuals.

  • Personality is based on heredity, upbringing, experience and environment, and is essentially fixed. Attitudes are tendencies to respond in a particular manner and can be changed — which is what CRM training targets.
  • Behaviour is the outward product of personality and attitude, and it is adaptable.
  • Synergy: collective performance exceeds the sum of the individuals — 1 + 1 > 2.
  • Authority gradients: autocratic, laissez-faire, synergistic. The synergistic cockpit is the goal; the main risk of a laissez-faire cockpit is inversion of authority.
  • Primary functions of leadership: information flow, coordination, motivation and decisions.
  • Effective communication means the message is transmitted with minimal change. Feedback is the element confirming that a message was received and understood.
  • Intra-personal conflict is conflict within one person; a cockpit disagreement over, say, smoking is best discussed during the debriefing, not in flight.
  • Procedures give procedural consistency. A good checklist is standardised, short and understandable.
  • Monitoring includes the detection of unusual conditions. Passive monitoring is watching without analysing — the failure mode. Mode awareness is understanding which automation modes are active and how the aircraft will respond.
  • Civil aviation is characterised by an open knowledge and reporting culture.
