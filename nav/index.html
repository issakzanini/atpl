The Earth, Direction & Distance

Two formulas — departure and convergency — generate most of the calculation questions in this subject.

Constants worth memorising
1° of latitude60 NM
1 minute of latitude1 NM
1 NM6080 ft / 1852 m
1 statute mile5280 ft
Earth's compression≈ 1/298
Sun's rate of travel15° of longitude / hour

Great circle vs rhumb line

  • Great circle — the shortest distance between two points; its plane passes through the centre of the Earth. Its direction changes continuously except along a meridian or the equator.
  • Rhumb line — a line of constant direction, cutting every meridian at the same angle. Longer than the great circle, and always lies on the equatorial side of it.
  • Meridians and the equator are both great circles and rhumb lines. Parallels of latitude (except the equator) are rhumb lines only.

The two formulas

  • Departure — the east–west distance along a parallel:
    Distance (NM) = ch.long (min) × cos(latitude)
  • Convergency — the angle at which two meridians converge:
    Convergency = ch.long (°) × sin(mean latitude)
  • Convergency therefore changes as the sine of latitude: zero at the equator, maximum at the poles.
  • Conversion angle = ½ convergency — the difference between the great circle and rhumb line track.
Worked example — departure
What is the distance along 60°N between 25°W and 85°W?

Change of longitude = 85 − 25 = 60° = 3600 minutes
Distance = 3600 × cos 60° = 3600 × 0.5 = 1800 NM

Same method, 10°E to 40°E on 60°N: 30° = 1800 min × 0.5 = 900 NM

061 02

Magnetism & Direction

Variation, deviation and the directive force. Learn the conversion chain once and never lose a mark here.

The conversion chain
True  —(variation)→  Magnetic  —(deviation)→  Compass

West is best, East is least — going from True to Compass, add West and subtract East.
Reverse the signs when going from Compass back to True.

Example: TH 320°, VAR 5°W, DEV 2°E
MH = 320 + 5 = 325°  →  CH = 325 − 2 = 323°

Definitions

  • Variation — the angle between True North and Magnetic North. Maximum value 180°. It varies slowly over time.
  • Deviation — the angle between Magnetic North and Compass North, caused by the aircraft's own magnetism. It changes with heading because the aircraft's undesired poles move relative to the Earth's field.
  • Isogonals join points of equal variation; they converge at the geographic and magnetic poles.
  • An agonic line joins points of zero variation.
  • True North is defined by the orientation of the local meridian.
  • Magnetic North is the direction of the horizontal component of the Earth's field.

The compass near the poles

  • The Earth's field has a horizontal and a vertical component. Only the horizontal component provides the directive force that aligns the needle.
  • With increasing magnetic latitude the field dips more steeply, so the horizontal component weakens and the directive force decreases — the compass becomes sluggish and finally useless.
  • At the magnetic poles a freely suspended needle stands vertical: there is no horizontal component at all.
  • The long-term periodic change in the Earth's field shows up as the slow movement of the magnetic poles.

061 03

Time

Solar versus sidereal, and why the length of the day is not constant.

  • Sidereal day — one rotation relative to a distant star. Constant duration, about 23 h 56 min.
  • Apparent solar day — the period between two successive transits of the true sun over the same meridian. It is longer than the sidereal day because the Earth's rotation and its orbital motion are in the same direction — the Earth must turn a little further to face the sun again.
  • Its length is not constant, because the Earth's orbital speed varies around its elliptical orbit and because of the inclination of the ecliptic.
  • The mean sun is a fiction that travels at a constant rate, giving Local Mean Time. At 0000 LMT the mean sun is in transit with the observer's anti-meridian.
  • Standard time is set by the legal authorities of a country and does not necessarily follow the 15° zones.
  • The main reason day and night vary in length through the year is the inclination of the ecliptic to the equator — the 23.5° tilt.
  • Near the equinoxes the influence of latitude on daylight duration is at its smallest.
  • Crossing the International Date Line from the east: you lose one day.
Worked example — standard time
Ecuador is UTC−5. It is 0325 UTC on 21 January.
0325 − 5 h = 2225 on 20 January

061 04

Charts & Projections

Two projections carry the whole topic. What matters is what a straight line means on each.

Direct Mercator

  • Meridians are parallel, equally spaced, vertical straight lines.
  • Rhumb lines are straight; great circles curve concave to the equator.
  • Scale is correct at the equator and expands as the secant of latitude.
  • Conformal, but useless near the poles.

Lambert conformal conic

  • Chart convergence is constant and does not change with latitude; it equals ch.long × sin(parallel of origin).
  • Earth convergency is most accurately represented at the parallel of origin.
  • Scale is constant along a parallel and correct along the two standard parallels.
  • Great circles that are not meridians are curves concave to the parallel of origin; a straight line approximates a great circle best near the parallel of origin.
  • The angular difference between initial and final true track on a straight line equals the chart convergence.
  • It must be processed mathematically to obtain conformality.
Worked example — scale
A chart scale of 1:20 000 means 1 unit on the chart is 20 000 of the same units on the Earth.
5 cm × 20 000 = 100 000 cm = 1000 m

Worked examples from the bank
Rate of descent, 3° glide, GS 175 kt:
RoD = GS × 5 = 175 × 5 = 875 ft/min

Rate of descent, 6.5 % glide, GS 175 kt:
RoD = 0.065 × 175 × 101.3 = ≈ 1137 ft/min

Distance to threshold, altitude 1400 ft, elevation 500 ft, 3° slope:
Height above threshold = 900 ft; at 300 ft per NM → 3 NM

Crosswind, RWY 32, wind 350/30:
Angle 30° → 30 × 0.5 = 15 kt

Headwind, RWY 32, wind 250/30:
Angle 70° → 30 × cos 70° = 30 × 0.34 = ≈ 10 kt

Speed and Mach

  • Mach = TAS / local speed of sound, and the LSS depends on temperature only: a = 39 × √T(K) kt.
  • Descending at constant TAS: the air warms, the LSS rises, so Mach decreases.
  • OAT decreasing at constant TAS: the LSS falls, so Mach increases.
Two answers in this bank do not add up

"Climb 20 000 ft in the next 50 NM at GS 300 kt" — the printed answer is 1000 ft/min. At 300 kt you cover 50 NM in 10 minutes, so the requirement is 2000 ft/min. "TT 281°, VAR 7°E, drift 10° left" — the printed answer is 185°. Drift left means you head right of track, so TH = 281 + 10 = 291°, and MH = 291 − 7 = 284°. Use the method, not the printed number.

Sample questions · fuel policy
What does trip fuel cover?

Fuel used between take-off and landing at the destination

What does taxi fuel cover?

All pre-departure operations — it does not include taxiing at the arrival airport

How much is contingency fuel?

5 % of trip fuel, or 5 minutes holding, whichever is greater

What is the final reserve for a jet, and for a piston aeroplane?

30 minutes holding at 1500 ft for jets; 45 minutes of cruise for pistons

What happens if you use final reserve fuel?

It constitutes a fuel emergency — planning to use it is prohibited

When is additional fuel required?

Only when trip + contingency + alternate + final reserve is insufficient to proceed to an alternate — for example isolated aerodrome or ETOPS operations

What is extra fuel?

Fuel added at the commander's discretion

033 03

PSR, ETP & the Planning Formulas

Two points on a route that answer two different questions: how far can I go and still get back, and where does it stop mattering which way I turn.

Flight planning formulas
Air distance / ground distanceNAM/TAS = NGM/GS
Ground milesNGM = NAM + (WC × T/60)
Mach numberTAS / LSS
Local speed of sound39 × √T(K) kt
VHF range1.23 × √h (ft) NM
PSR distanceE × O × H / (O + H)

NAM is a nautical air mile — distance through the air mass. NGM is a nautical ground mile. The wind component converts one into the other, and the ratio NAM/TAS = NGM/GS is how you move between them in a fuel calculation.

Sample questions · PSR & ETP
What is the relationship between NAM, NGM, TAS and GS?

NAM / TAS = NGM / GS

How do you convert air miles to ground miles?

NGM = NAM + (wind component × time in minutes / 60)

What is the PSR formula, and what do the letters mean?

Time to PSR = E × H / (O + H). E = safe endurance, O = groundspeed out, H = groundspeed homeTime to the PSR is computed with all-engines TAS.

What is the ETP formula?

Distance to ETP = D × H / (O + H), where D is the total distance

What is the VHF range formula?

Range (NM) = 1.23 × √height in feet

How do you calculate Mach number, and what does the local speed of sound depend on?

Mach = TAS / LSS; the LSS depends on temperature only

Mass & Balance Basics

Three definitions and one subtraction. Free marks.

The mass chain
Dry Operating Mass (DOM) = aircraft + crew + equipment, no fuel, no traffic load
Zero Fuel Mass (ZFM) = DOM + traffic load
Take-Off Mass (TOM) = DOM + traffic load + take-off fuel  =  ZFM + fuel
Landing Mass = TOM − trip fuel

Example: TOM 117 000 kg, traffic load 18 000 kg, fuel 46 000 kg
DOM = 117 000 − 18 000 − 46 000 = 53 000 kg
