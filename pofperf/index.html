081 01

Lift, Drag & the Drag Curve

One graph answers about forty questions in this bank. Learn it properly and the climb, cruise, glide and endurance answers all fall out of it.

The lift equation

L = CL · ½ρV2 · S. Lift varies with the square of the speed, so at the same angle of attack, flying twice as fast gives four times the lift. The critical angle of attack is fixed — it does not change with mass, altitude, or configuration. That is why angle of attack is the parameter the stall warning system uses.

The two drags

  • Induced drag — the price of making lift. Caused by spanwise flow: high pressure beneath the wing spills round the wing tip to the low-pressure upper surface, creating the tip vortex and a downwash that tilts the lift vector rearwards.
  • Di ∝ CL2, and since CL ∝ W/V2, induced drag ∝ W2/V2. So it decreases with speed and increases with mass, and it is greatest at VS0 — the lowest speed you can fly.
  • Reduce it by increasing aspect ratio, using an elliptical lift distribution, winglets, or washout.
  • Parasite drag (form + skin friction + interference) ∝ V2. It increases with speed and is independent of mass.
  • Boundary layer: friction drag is lower in the laminar layer, but the laminar layer separates more easily. The change-over point is the transition point; the point where the flow leaves the surface is the separation point. Separation is characterised by airflow reversal on the surface.
  • A laminar-flow profile's biggest benefit is low profile drag.
Speeds that are the same thing (jet)

Ground effect

Within half a wingspan of the surface the ground blocks the downwash. Less downwash means the induced angle of attack and induced drag both decrease, and at constant angle of attack the effective angle of attack increases, so lift rises. Practical consequences: floating in the flare, so ground effect increases the landing distance; and on leaving ground effect (climbing out), the effective angle of attack decreases, drag rises and the aeroplane may sink.

Stall & Stall Speeds

The stall always happens at the same angle of attack. Everything that "increases the stall speed" does so by requiring more lift, not by changing the critical AoA.

Bank 45°n 1.41 · V S ×1.19
Bank 60°n 2.00 · V S ×1.41
Formulaen = 1/cosφ · V S t = V S√n

Load factor n = L/W, and in a level coordinated turn it depends on bank angle only — not on speed, not on mass. Stall speed rises with the square root of the load factor.

What raises the stall speed

  • Increased mass, increased load factor (turning, pulling out of a dive, gusts), forward CG (the tail has to push down, so the wing carries more than the weight), ice, and reduced thrust (less vertical thrust component).
  • Not altitude: stall speed in IAS is essentially constant with altitude, only rising at very high altitude because of compressibility.
  • Lowest stall speed: light and aft CG.
  • Highest stall speed for a given configuration: maximum mass with the most forward CG.

081 03

High Speed Flight

Mach number, the swept wing, and the buffet boundary that decides how high you can actually go.

  • Local speed of sound depends on temperature only: a = 39 × √T(K) kt, or a = 661.5 × √(T/288.15).
  • M = TAS / LSS.
  • MCRIT is the free-stream Mach at which the flow somewhere on the wing first reaches M 1.0. Beyond it: shock waves, shock-induced separation, Mach buffet, drag rise, Mach tuck.
  • Swept wing: the chordwise component of the airflow is reduced, so MCRIT is higher. The costs are a lower CLmax, tip stall tendency and Dutch roll.
  • Constant CAS climb → TAS increases and Mach increases. Constant Mach climb to the tropopause → TAS decreases (falling temperature).
  • Constant Mach descent below the tropopause → CAS and TAS increase, angle of attack decreases, and the margin to low-speed buffet increases.
  • Crossover altitude — where the climb CAS schedule meets the Mach schedule. A higher CAS gives a lower crossover altitude; a lower CAS gives a higher one.

081 04

Climb, Glide & Turn

Climb performance is excess thrust; rate of climb is excess power; glide range is L/D. Three ideas, a dozen questions.

Climb formulae
Climb gradient(Thrust − Drag)/Weight ×100
Rate of climbexcess power / weight
Gradient definitionheight gained / horiz. distance
ROC from gradientgrad % × GS(kt) × 1.013
Glide distanceL/D × height
Absolute ceilingrate of climb = 0

V X and V Y

  • V X — best angle of climb: most height per unit of ground distance. Maximum excess thrust. For a jet this is V MD (maximum CL/CD). Use it for obstacle clearance.
  • V Y — best rate of climb: most height per unit of time. Maximum excess power. Always faster than V X.
  • With altitude the two converge; they meet at the absolute ceiling, where excess thrust and power are both zero.
  • Above the OEI ceiling, descend at the best angle of climb speed — the drift-down speed.

Wind and the flight path

  • Wind does not change the time to climb, or V X and V Y as airspeeds — those are air-mass quantities.
  • Wind does change the flight-path angle over the ground: a headwind steepens both the climb and the descent path and reduces the ground distance covered in the climb.
  • Climb/descent angle is air-mass related; flight-path angle is ground related.
  • For maximum range over the ground into a headwind, fly faster than the still-air maximum range speed. V X is unchanged.
  • Gliding: a tailwind increases the ground distance; a higher mass gives a higher best-glide speed but the same glide angle — and therefore maximum glide time comes from low mass.
Two definitions they love to swap

Climb angle is measured in degrees, climb gradient as a percentage — and because gradient is the tangent, for small angles the gradient number is always larger than the angle number (3° ≈ 5.2 %). Also note the bank's answer to "V Y is defined as…" reads "speed for best angle of descent": that is an error in the source. V Y is the speed for the best rate of climb.

Every CG question in the bank resolves the same way: an aft CG needs less download from the tail, so the wing carries less — and everything gets better except stability.

Moving the CG aftMoving the CG forward

An aeroplane whose CG is behind the centre of pressure needs the horizontal tail load to act upwards to balance. The CG is certified to move between defined limits; behind the aft limit, stability decreases and controllability increases — the aeroplane becomes divergent in pitch.

Flap setting — the trade-off

Going from more flap to less flap (e.g. 15° → 5°) gives a longer take-off distance but a better climb gradient: less drag once airborne, more runway to accelerate to a higher speed. So a low flap setting is what you want with a long runway, distant obstacles, high field elevation and high temperature, and it increases the obstacle limit while decreasing the runway limit.

Reduced (flex / assumed temperature) thrust

Must be at least 75 % of full take-off thrust. Its purpose is to reduce engine wear and maintenance cost and improve engine life. It may not be used when the runway is contaminated, when anti-skid is unserviceable, or when windshear is reported.

032 04

Cruise & Range

Specific range, optimum altitude, cost index, step climb. All four are the same idea seen from different angles: miles per kilo of fuel.

Cruise definitions
Specific rangeSR = TAS / fuel flow
SFC (jet)fuel flow per unit thrust
Optimum altitudealtitude of maximum SR
Long Range Cruise99 % of max SR, faster
Cost indextime costs / fuel costs
Max range speed (jet)min SFC, ≈1.32 V MD
  • Optimum altitude increases as mass decreases — which is exactly why the step climb exists. It also increases with decreasing Mach number.
  • As fuel burns off during a long flight, specific range and optimum altitude both increase.
  • At maximum range cruise, as mass reduces, IAS decreases and drag decreases.
  • Long Range Cruise deliberately gives up 1 % of specific range to buy a useful increase in speed — the classic definition is "the speed which offers 99 % of the maximum possible fuel mileage", on the high-speed side.
  • Cost index = time-related costs ÷ fuel cost. CI 0 = maximum range (minimum fuel); a high CI pushes the speed up towards VMO/MMO.
  • A step climb at high altitude can be prevented by buffet onset at load factors above 1 g — see the buffet boundary above.
  • Fuel flow vs TAS: fuel flow decreases up to the maximum endurance speed, then increases.
  • The flight plan cruise speed entry is a true airspeed.

Worked example — bank question 249
Landing distance available 3000 m, runway wet, commercial jet.

Dry requirement: ALD ≤ 0.60 × LDA
Wet requirement: divide that by a further 1.15
ALD ≤ (3000 × 0.60) / 1.15 = 1800 / 1.15 = 1565 m
Equivalently: 3000 ÷ 1.92 = 1562 m. Same answer, easier arithmetic.
Worked example — wind limits, bank question 179
Required: at least 10 kt headwind; maximum 35 kt crosswind; wind 60° off the runway.

Headwind = V × cos 60° = 0.5 V  →  0.5 V ≥ 10  →  V ≥ 20 kt
Crosswind = V × sin 60° = 0.87 V  →  0.87 V ≤ 35  →  V ≤ 40 kt
Answer: 20 kt minimum, 40 kt maximum
  • Hydroplaning technique: a positive, firm touchdown, then full reverse and brakes as early as possible. Do not float — ground effect already lengthens the landing.
  • Dynamic hydroplaning speed ≈ 9 × √(tyre pressure in psi) kt.
  • Raising the screen height from 50 ft to 67 ft increases the landing distance — you spend the extra height in the air.
  • If no AFM data exists for a wet runway, the required landing distance on a dry runway must be increased by 15 %.
