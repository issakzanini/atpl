Direct Reading Compass

Nineteen questions in this bank, and all of them come from one fact: the magnet is pendulously suspended and the Earth's field dips.

Compass to True — reverse the signs
Compass Heading 124°, Deviation −3°, Variation 4°W
MH = 124 − 3 = 121°  →  TH = 121 − 4 = 117°
Going Compass → True, subtract West. Going True → Compass, add West.
  • Turning right through north the compass under-indicates the turn, and liquid swirl increases the effect. Through south it over-indicates.
  • Hard iron magnetism is permanent; soft iron is temporary. Compass calibration (swinging) reduces deviation and determines it on any heading.
  • A flux valve senses the direction of the Earth's field for a remote reading (gyromagnetic) compass.

022 03

Gyroscopes

Two properties, three instruments, and the wander that has to be corrected.

Properties

  • Rigidity in space and precession. Rigidity is improved by increasing RPM and concentrating the mass at the periphery.
  • A gyro precesses when a force is applied at right angles to its rotational axis, and the result appears 90° further round in the direction of rotation.
  • Without external action, a free gyro's axis is fixed with reference to space.

Wander

  • Apparent wander (earth rate) — the Earth turns beneath the gyro. It varies with latitude: 15 × sin(lat) per hour, so it is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles.
  • Transport wander — caused by the convergence of meridians as the aircraft moves; it depends on ground speed, true track and mean latitude.
  • Drift is wander in the horizontal plane; topple is wander in the vertical plane.

The three instruments

InstrumentGyroSpin axis
Directional gyro2 DOFHorizontal
Artificial horizon2 DOF + erectionVertical (local)
Turn indicatorRate gyro, 1 DOFParallel to the pitch axis
  • An air-driven artificial horizon uses four pendulous vanes for erection. Acceleration on take-off gives a false nose-up; deceleration on landing a false nose-down.
  • After the first 90° of a turn at constant pitch and bank, a classic artificial horizon shows too much nose-up and too little bank.
  • Rate of turn depends on bank angle and airspeed. Rate one is 3° per second.
  • Needle right, ball left = turning right with not enough bank (slipping in).
Apparent drift

022 04

Inertial Systems

Accelerometers plus integration. The whole subject is knowing how many times you integrate.

The integration chain
Acceleration  —(integrate once)→  Speed  —(integrate again)→  Distance / position

Instantaneous speed needs one integration plus the initial speed.
Instantaneous position needs double integration plus initial position and speed.
The integration process is, in effect, a multiplication by time.
  • An INS is self-contained — it needs no ground signals. Its position error increases with time, and drift is the error with the greatest effect.
  • Alignment aligns the platform to the local vertical and true north. The sequence is local vertical, true north, latitude, latitude comparison.
  • Once aligned it outputs N/S and E/W acceleration, attitude and true heading.
  • TAS input is required only to give a wind vector readout — so if the ADC fails, the IRS no longer provides wind direction and speed.
  • Strapdown means the gyros and accelerometers are fixed to the aircraft structure rather than on a stabilised platform. It gives a shorter alignment and is more reliable.
  • A ring laser gyro measures angular movement about its sensitive axis, using the frequency difference between two counter-rotating laser beams.
  • IRS ATT mode is a back-up providing attitude and heading only.
  • IRS outputs include true and magnetic heading, ground speed, latitude and longitude, true track, present position and attitude.

022 05

Automatic Flight & FMS

What the FMS knows, where it gets its position, and what the autothrottle is doing.

  • The FMS is a global 4D flight management system: lateral and vertical navigation and guidance plus performance management.
  • The navigation database holds waypoints, SIDs and STARs, runway approaches, airport reference data and magnetic variation. Flight-envelope data lives in the separate performance database.
  • The FMC position is a computed position from multiple sources — IRS, DME, VOR, LOC, GPS. DME/DME gives the most accurate radio fix.
  • VNAV vertical command outputs are flight path angle and speed target.
  • The flight director gives the attitude needed to join a desired path.
  • During an automatic approach the autothrottle holds IAS (SPEED mode); it can also be used for take-off, and it protects the envelope through reversion modes.
  • A semi-automatic landing system disconnects at the decision height. During a CAT II automatic approach, height comes from the radio altimeter.
  • The stick shaker is part of the stall warning system; a stall protection system takes over and lowers the nose.
  • The angle of attack vane feeds the ADC and is protected by electric heating.

EGPWS / TAWS

  • The EGPWS has its own world terrain database; the Terrain Look-Ahead function uses an electronic map giving ground elevation.
  • Functions: Terrain Clearance Floor, Terrain Look Ahead Alerting, Terrain Alerting and Display. Display colours: green, amber, red and blue.

TCAS II

  • Works on transponder replies — secondary radar principle, independent of ATC, and no advisory at all against an aircraft without a transponder.
  • Range is measured by the time between interrogation and reply; the collision logic is based on time, not distance.

  • Intruder with no altitude reporting → traffic advisory only.
  • Display: TA = amber solid circle, RA = red filled square.
  • Preventive RA = keep the vertical speed within limits. Corrective RA = change the vertical speed. Follow the RA immediately, and if it conflicts with ATC, follow the RA and tell ATC.

062 01

Radio Propagation

Wave behaviour decides the range of every aid that follows.

  • Modulation is the addition of information onto a carrier wave. Keying is interrupting the carrier to make dots and dashes.
  • Phase angle is a fraction of one wavelength expressed in degrees. Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field oscillation.
  • Refraction is a change in the direction of a wave caused by a change in its speed.
  • The simplest antenna is a dipole — a wire half a wavelength long. Directional antennas: loop, parabolic, slotted planar and helical.
  • VHF and above propagate as space waves — line of sight. Range is limited by the curvature of the Earth and line of sight, and depends on the height of both antennas. It is degraded by terrain and obstacles, and by tropospheric conditions and atmospheric absorption.
  • HF travels as sky waves day and night, with a very small ground-wave range. The skip distance increases with a higher frequency and a higher reflecting layer.
  • Coastal refraction mainly affects the NDB.
  • VOR uses metric wavelengths (VHF).

062 02

NDB, VOR & DME

The three conventional aids, and the slant range trap.

  • NDB is the ground equipment; ADF is the airborne equipment. Passage of a locator is shown by a 180° needle swing of the ADF indicator.
  • To plot a VOR bearing you apply the variation at the VOR. On an RMI: apply variation at the aircraft position for an NDB, at the beacon position for a VOR.
  • CDI full deflection = 10° or more off the radial.
  • VOR transmissions are affected by uneven propagation over irregular ground. A TVOR is the aid with deliberately limited range.
  • DME measures slant range, so overhead a station it reads your height, not zero.
  • Accurate DME groundspeed requires flying directly towards or away from the station.
  • In tracking mode the interrogator transmits a low pulse-pair rate; interrogation does not start until pulse-pairs from the tuned station are received.
  • At a co-located VOR/DME the DME has the same ident but a different tone.

062 03

ILS & Markers

Two beams, three markers, and a set of frequencies and colours that come up every time.

  • The ILS produces a radiation pattern amplitude-modulated by 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals; the difference in depth of modulation (DDM) increases linearly with angular displacement from the centreline.
  • False glide paths arise from multiple lobes of the radiation pattern in the vertical plane.
  • Scalloping is rapid side-to-side indicator movement that is not flyable.
  • FM-immunity filters protect the localizer only.
  • Maximum safe deviation below the glide path is half scale — on a 5-dot indicator, 2.5 dots fly-up. Beyond that, go around.

  • A GPS position line is obtained by timing how long a satellite transmission takes to reach the receiver; the primary information takes the form of spheres with the aircraft at the centre.
  • Four satellites give latitude, longitude, altitude and time.
  • The navigation message carries almanac data and satellite status, plus orbit and clock references. Clock corrections correct satellite time.
  • GPS satellites use helical antennas because the signal has circular polarization. Airframe shadowing may prevent reception.
  • SBAS broadcasts corrections from geostationary satellites, split into clock, ephemeris and ionospheric errors. GBAS covers roughly 30 km. ABAS/AAIM uses GNSS plus additional sensors such as an IRS.
  • Altitude datum for IFR on airways using GPS remains barometric.
  • RNAV vs RNP: both require accuracy checks, but only RNP has performance monitoring and alerting requirements.
  • System performance is defined by accuracy, integrity and continuity.
  • "X" in RNAV X or RNP X is the lateral navigation accuracy in NM achieved at least 95 % of the flight time.
  • B-RNAV / RNAV 5: ±5 NM for 95 % of the time. RNP 1: ±1 NM lateral and longitudinal. Precision RNAV requires RNP 1.
  • 2D = horizontal only. 3D = horizontal plus vertical. 4D adds time.
  • A phantom station is a waypoint defined by a radial and DME distance from a VOR/DME. INS is the example of a self-contained RNAV system.
  • If the GPS position differs unacceptably from conventional systems, or RAIM is unavailable, the flight may continue using other certified navigation systems.

What is the difference between RNAV and RNP?

Both require accuracy checks, but only RNP has performance monitoring requirements

Which factors define RNAV or RNP system performance requirements?

Accuracy, integrity and continuity

What does "X" mean in RNAV X or RNP X?

The lateral navigation accuracy in NM, achieved at least 95 % of the flight time

What track-keeping accuracy does Basic RNAV require?

±5 NM or better for 95 % of the flight time

What does RNP 1 require?

±1 NM both lateral and longitudinal for 95 % of the flight time

What capability does a 2D, a 3D and a 4D RNAV system have?

2D horizontal; 3D adds vertical; 4D adds time

What is a phantom station?

A waypoint defined by a radial and a DME distance from a VOR/DME station

INS
